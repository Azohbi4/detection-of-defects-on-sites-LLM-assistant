## 1. Concrete Cracking (Fissuration du béton)

### Description and Classification
Cracks in reinforced concrete bridge elements are classified by their physical appearance, orientation, depth, and mechanism of occurrence.
- Structural cracking: Caused by external loads (bending, shear, torsion) exceeding the tensile strength of the concrete. Typical patterns include diagonal shear cracks near bridge supports and transverse flexural cracks at maximum moment sections.
- Non-structural cracking: Includes plastic shrinkage cracking, drying shrinkage cracking, thermal contraction, and chemical reaction cracks (such as alkali-silica reaction or freeze-thaw cycles).

### Field Inspection Procedure
Bridge inspectors must document:
- Orientation: Transverse, longitudinal, vertical, diagonal, or map/pattern cracking.
- Location and extent: Component affected, linear length, density of cracks per unit area.
- Width measurement: Measured using an optical crack comparator or graduated gauge. Cracks are classified as hairline (< 0.1 mm), narrow (0.1 to 0.3 mm), medium (0.3 to 1.0 mm), or wide (> 1.0 mm).
- Active vs. dormant status: Visual observation of dynamic movement, presence of active water seepage, or deployment of crack monitors (mechanical tell-tale or optical gauges) over seasonal temperature cycles.

### Corrective Actions and Maintenance Recommendations
- Narrow, dormant cracks: Apply penetrating silane/siloxane hydrophobic sealers to prevent moisture and chloride intrusion into the concrete matrix.
- Structural or medium-to-wide active cracks: Conduct a comprehensive structural evaluation by a qualified bridge engineer. For dormant structural cracks wider than 0.3 mm, high-pressure epoxy resin injection is recommended to restore tensile continuity. For active cracks subjected to dynamic live loads, elastomeric joint sealing or flexible polyurethane routing and sealing must be utilized.

## 2. Delamination and Spalling (Décollement et éclatement du béton)

### Description and Mechanism
- Delamination: A subsurface plane of separation in the concrete, commonly oriented parallel to the surface. It is predominantly initiated by the volumetric expansion of corroding reinforcing steel (rust products occupy up to 6 times the volume of the original steel).
- Spall: A fracture or detachment of a concrete fragment from the parent concrete body, leaving a rough depression or cavity. Spalling represents the advanced failure stage following delamination.

### Field Inspection Protocol
- Visual signs: Depressions with jagged edges, exposed underlying aggregate, and concrete fragments at risk of falling.
- Tactile and acoustic detection: Sounding with a bridge inspector's hammer (yielding a dull, hollow thud rather than a clear ringing sound) or chain dragging on horizontal bridge decks to locate unseen delaminations.
- Measurement: Length, width, depth of cavity, and condition of remaining concrete periphery.

### Corrective Actions and Repair Procedures
- Immediate safety measures: Scale and mechanically scale/purge all loose or unsound concrete fragments to prevent hazardous debris from falling onto roadways or waterways below.
- Concrete restoration: Saw-cut the perimeter of the spalled area to a minimum depth of 15 to 20 mm with perpendicular edges (avoiding feathered edges). Chipping away damaged concrete to at least 20 mm behind exposed reinforcing bars. Clean the cavity substrate with high-pressure water blast, apply a bonding agent, and reconstruct using structural repair mortars or polymer-modified cementitious patching materials.

## 3. Exposed Reinforcing Steel (Armatures apparentes)

### Description
Exposed reinforcing bars occur when the concrete cover is completely lost through severe spalling, impact, mechanical abrasion, or insufficient initial construction cover. The steel rebar is in direct contact with ambient oxygen, moisture, and deicing chemicals.

### Field Inspection Protocol
- Verification of concrete cover depth around the affected periphery.
- Inspection of steel condition: Note presence of laminar rust scales, pitting corrosion, or brittle microfractures.
- Cross-sectional measurement: Measure the remaining bar diameter with a vernier caliper to calculate effective percentage section loss.

### Corrective Actions and Repair
- Minor section loss (< 10-15% cross-section reduction): Abrasive blast clean the rebar to bare metal (SSPC-SP 10 / NACE 2 near-white metal). Apply a protective zinc-rich epoxy coating or cementitious corrosion inhibitor to the steel before placing concrete patch material.
- Significant section loss (> 15-20% cross-section reduction): Immediate referral to a licensed structural engineer. Splicing of supplemental reinforcing bars, mechanical rebar couplers, or external carbon fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) structural strengthening is required.

## 4. Corrosion Staining (Traces de corrosion et rouille)

### Description
Corrosion stains appear as reddish-brown or yellowish-orange discoloration streaks on the external concrete surface, often emanating from hairline cracks, joints, or pores.

### Significance and Diagnosis
Surface rust stains are a critical non-destructive diagnostic sign of active sub-surface corrosion of steel reinforcements or embedded metallic components. They indicate that the concrete passivating alkaline layer (pH > 12.5) has been compromised by carbonation or high chloride concentrations reaching the rebar level.

### Recommendations
- Investigate the specific source of staining: verify if it originates from an external metal fixture (drainage pipes, railings) or internal reinforcing bars.
- Perform half-cell potential mapping (ASTM C876) or carbonation depth testing with phenolphthalein indicator.
- Remediate localized water ingress and apply surface penetrating corrosion-inhibiting chemicals (migrating corrosion inhibitors).

## 5. Efflorescence and Moisture Infiltration (Efflorescence et infiltrations d'eau)

### Description and Chemical Mechanism
Efflorescence is a white, powdery crystalline deposit of soluble salts (predominantly calcium carbonate) on the concrete surface. Water infiltrates through microcracks or pores, dissolves free calcium hydroxide (lime) from the hydrated cement paste, migrates to the surface, and reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide to form insoluble calcium carbonate.

### Diagnostic Value
Efflorescence is undeniable physical evidence of continuous or intermittent moisture movement through the concrete element. It frequently coincides with deck membrane failures, malfunctioning expansion joints, or poor deck drainage.

### Corrective Actions and Maintenance
- Water source elimination: Inspect and repair bridge deck waterproofing membranes, clean blocked scuppers, and reseal expansion joints.
- Surface remediation: Dry brushing or mild pressurized water washing. Avoid aggressive acid etching that can compromise the concrete matrix. If no structural cracking or internal disintegration is present, efflorescence itself does not diminish structural capacity but serves as a clear warning indicator for future corrosion risks.
